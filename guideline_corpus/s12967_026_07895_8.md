Abstract
Objective This work proposes a unified framework for causal digital twins, integrating Structural Causal Models
(SCMs), the Potential Outcomes Framework, and reinforcement learning. The objective is to enable individualized
counterfactual reasoning and dynamic treatment policy optimization, moving beyond prediction toward actionable,
ethical, and adaptive decision support.
Materials and methods This work formalizes causal digital twins by embedding causal inference principles
into digital architectures, modeling both observed and counterfactual outcomes for each patient. Individualized
Treatment Effects (ITE) are estimated through causal modeling, and sequential decision-making is optimized using
reinforcement learning techniques. This work is a methodological and conceptual research contribution. We propose
a unified framework for the construction and evaluation of clinical digital twins and a practical checklist covering
validation, safety, and ethical requirements. No new dataset or algorithmic benchmark is introduced.
Results This work discusses methodological requirements, learning objectives, evaluation strategies, and ethical
considerations necessary for deploying causal digital twins in real-world clinical contexts. The proposed framework
allows for accurate estimation of counterfactual outcomes, personalized treatment effect modeling, and dynamic
policy learning over time.
Discussion Through this integration, digital twins evolve from static predictors into active engines of individualized
intervention, providing a new paradigm for precision healthcare. Causal digital twins offer a transformative extension
of current digital twin technologies, merging causal reasoning with dynamic simulation to enable personalized,
counterfactual-driven clinical decision support.
Conclusion This work lays the methodological foundation for operationalizing causal digital twins in future AI-driven
healthcare systems, with profound implications for ethical, individualized, and adaptive medicine.

Theoretical framework
In contrast to purely predictive models, which estimate
what is likely to happen given observed data, causal inference aims to answer intervention-based questions such
as: “What would happen to this patient if we actively
changed the treatment?” [15].
For example, a predictive digital twin may learn that
patients receiving treatment A have worse outcomes
than those receiving treatment B. However, this association alone does not imply that treatment A is harmful, as
patients receiving A may be systematically sicker. Causal
inference explicitly models such confounding mechanisms and seeks to estimate outcomes under hypothetical interventions, answering questions such as “What
would the outcome have been for this same patient had
treatment B been given instead?”.
Structural causal models formalize this reasoning by separating observed associations from causal
effects, enabling digital twins to simulate counterfactual

Digital twin instantiation and simulation
1. State initialization:

Key causal and modeling assumptions
1. Consistency: the modeled interventions correspond to real clinical beta-blocker regimens.
2. Conditional exchangeability: given observed covariates, treatment assignment is independent of potential outcomes.
3. Positivity: similar patients exist in historical data for both dose strategies.
4. Correct model specification: physiological and statistical submodels adequately approximate short-term cardiovascular responses.
5. Temporal stability: treatment effect mechanisms remain stable over the modeled horizon.

Data preprocessing and representation learning
Data preprocessing transforms raw data into structured
inputs suitable for causal modeling. Key steps include:
• Missing data imputation: e.g., using mean
imputation, multiple imputation, or model-based
techniques;
• Normalization and encoding: scaling continuous
variables, one-hot encoding categorical variables, or

• Dimensionality reduction: applying methods like

complex covariate structures;
• Balancing covariates: via matching (e.g., propensity
score matching), reweighting (e.g., IPTW), or
learning balanced representations (e.g., adversarial
domain adaptation).
Representation learning ensures that models retain relevant causal information while simplifying high-dimensional input spaces.

Deployment in healthcare settings
For practical utility, digital twins must integrate into realworld clinical workflows:
– Interoperability: support for standards such as HL7

– Explainability: use interpretability tools like SHAP,

– Real-time inference: models should offer low-latency
predictions to inform clinical action;
– Continual learning: systems should adapt as new
data becomes available, ensuring model relevance.
Ethical oversight and fairness
Ethical deployment of digital twins in healthcare requires
rigorous governance:
– Bias audits: systematically evaluate performance
across demographics (e.g., sex, race, age);
– Transparency: clearly document data sources, model
assumptions, and design choices;
– Accountability: ensure human oversight over
automated recommendations, especially in critical
care settings.
Developing and deploying causal digital twins in healthcare raises significant ethical considerations that must be
addressed systematically within the methodology.
First, transparency and explainability are essential to
ensure that clinicians and patients understand the basis
of treatment recommendations. Models must integrate
interpretability tools, such as SHAP or ICE plots, to make
the causal mechanisms and individualized treatment
effects comprehensible to non-technical users.
Second, fairness and bias auditing must be an integral part of model validation. Digital twins should be
evaluated across demographic subgroups (e.g., age, sex,

Page 12 of 18
ethnicity, socioeconomic status) to detect and mitigate
disparate impacts. Strategies such as subgroup-specific
performance metrics, fairness-constrained optimization,
and bias mitigation techniques should be employed to
ensure equitable model behavior.
Third, privacy and data protection are paramount.
Given that digital twins simulate sensitive health trajectories, robust measures must be implemented to safeguard
patient data. Approaches such as data de-identification,
federated learning, and differential privacy mechanisms
can be used to minimize the risks associated with centralized data storage and model training.
Then, the deployment of causal digital twins must
uphold the principle of human oversight. Clinical decision-making should not be fully delegated to automated
systems; rather, digital twins should serve as advisory
tools that augment, but do not replace, physician judgment. Clear accountability frameworks must be established to delineate the roles and responsibilities of model
developers, clinical users, and institutional governance
bodies.

The development of digital twins as causal digital twins
marks a transformative step in personalized medicine
and data-driven healthcare [5, 16, 17]. Unlike traditional
predictive models, causal digital twins not only forecast
likely outcomes but also simulate alternative scenarios,
allowing clinicians to answer “what if” questions with
greater confidence [1]. One of the principal strengths of
causal digital twins lies in their ability to estimate individualized treatment effects [18]. This capacity enables
tailored medical decisions that go beyond average treatment effects by accounting for the heterogeneity in
patient characteristics and responses (Table 2) [19].

By incorporating formal causal frameworks such as

learning architectures, ranging from neural networks to
tree-based methods [7], which allows for their scalable
deployment across large and complex clinical datasets.
Their compatibility with interpretability tools, such as
SHAP values or ICE plots [21, 22], supports transparency
and builds clinician trust in decision-support systems.
Despite these advantages, several challenges remain.
The reliability of a digital twin model depends heavily on the quality, completeness, and granularity of the
data it uses [23]. Missing data, measurement error, and
unmeasured confounding can significantly compromise
model performance [24]. Moreover, because counterfactual outcomes are inherently unobservable, validating
these predictions remains methodologically difficult [13].
Although synthetic benchmarks and simulated data provide partial solutions [25, 26], they cannot fully substitute
real-world evidence. Another challenge is the computational complexity involved in training robust models,
particularly those that rely on generative or adversarial
architectures [27]. Furthermore, models trained in one
healthcare setting or population may not generalize well
to others without further calibration or domain adaptation [28]. Finally, deploying digital twins in clinical environments raises important regulatory, legal, and ethical
questions [29, 30]. These include concerns about model
transparency, fairness across patient populations, and the
degree of human oversight required in high-stakes decision-making [31].
Looking forward, several promising directions are
emerging. The integration of digital twins into real-time
clinical decision support systems could bring simulated
insights directly to the point of care [32]. The development of longitudinal and dynamic twins, capable
of updating as patient trajectories evolve, may further

Conclusion
Causal digital twins represent a pivotal advancement in
precision medicine, enabling not only the prediction of
future health trajectories but also the simulation of individualized counterfactual scenarios. By integrating structural causal models, potential outcomes frameworks, and
reinforcement learning into dynamic digital architectures, this work offers a unified foundation for optimizing personalized treatment decisions. While challenges
related to data quality, counterfactual validation, and fairness remain, the methodological synthesis proposed here
moves digital twins beyond prediction toward actionable
and adaptive decision support systems, paving the way
for the next generation of AI-driven healthcare. Moreover, while causal digital twins offer powerful counterfactual insights, their clinical impact ultimately depends
on careful validation of causal assumptions and responsible integration into human-centered decision-making
workflows.
